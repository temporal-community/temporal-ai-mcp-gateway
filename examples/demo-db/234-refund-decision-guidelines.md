## Context

Refund decisions are made case by case with no written policy. The result is inconsistency that customers notice and sometimes exploit, and new team members who spend their first months asking a colleague every time.

## Proposal

Written guidelines with clear discretion bounds and a path for the cases outside them.

- Automatic approval within defined bounds: amount, time since charge, and account history
- A documented list of circumstances where refusal is correct, with the wording to use
- Discretion above the bounds, requiring a second person and a recorded reason
- Quarterly review of the recorded reasons, which is where the guidelines get better

## Scope

Self-serve refunds. Enterprise contracts have terms and are handled by the account team.

## Risks

Written guidelines make the boundary visible, and a visible boundary is a target. The discretion path exists partly for this reason, and the recorded reasons are the input that keeps the bounds current as behavior adapts.

## Measurement

Decision consistency measured on matched cases, time to a refund decision, refund rate, and discretion cases as a share of the total.

## Data We Have

Eighteen months of refund decisions sit in the support tool, but the reason field is free text and about a third of it says "customer request". That is enough to set opening bounds and not enough to defend them. Amount and time since charge are reliable; account history is not, because repeat refunds are only linked when the same email was used.

- Median refunded amount sits well below any automatic bound drafted so far
- Roughly one decision in nine would fall outside those bounds
- Refusals reversed after escalation are frequent enough to be their own category

## Staffing

The guidelines are cheap to write and expensive to keep. The quarterly review needs an owner who reads the recorded reasons rather than counting them, which is most of a day per quarter on the support side plus a finance reviewer for the discretion cases. Unless that owner is named, the bounds drift from behavior and the written policy becomes the thing people route around.

## What Would Change Our Mind

If two quarters of recorded reasons show discretion cases clustering on one circumstance, fold that circumstance into the automatic bounds rather than keep paying for second-person review. If the discretion share instead climbs past a quarter of all decisions, the bounds are drawn too tight and the policy is adding steps without adding consistency.
